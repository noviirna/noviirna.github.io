When people hear about "career break," they often assume the biggest challenge is keeping technical skills up to date, I personally find **that statement wasn't entirely relatable.**

## The Unplanned Hit The Brake Moment

My career break wasn't planned, and it happens not because of just a single reason. It happened when several priorities collided at once. Juggling a physical recovery after getting injured*[[read the blog here: "What a Long-Term Physical Injury Taught Me That No Career Ever Could"](/blog/2026/what-injury-recovery-journey-taught-me/)]*, managing caregiving responsibilities, and hunting for graduate scholarship opportunities. It was the moment when I decided to hit the brake just to stay afloat *[[read the blog here: "When Life Gives You Lemons, Run an ROI & Risk Analysis"](/blog/2026/when-life-gives-you-lemons/)]*. Software engineering simply faded into the background because other responsibilities needed a lot of my attention and I didn't have enough bandwidth for all of those at the same time.

After I recently decided to pivot back to pursuing my career instead of a scholarship, I kept finding myself reflecting about what I wanted to do next. And the closest answer I got was this: "Instead of immediately returning to software engineering, maybe it might be worth trying to explore other careers where my transferable skills might be valuable". So I researched different roles, & career possibilities, reflected on what kind of work energized me, tried to get to know myself again, learned stuffs just to upskill, all of it while kept an open mind.

## Re-discovering Curiosity

*I wasn't trying to convince myself to return to engineering* obviously; if anything, I was trying to understand whether I still wanted to go back or I was fully over it. Then something unexpected happened. As I built my portfolio hub, I explored some software engineering stuffs such as: experimenting with AI coding agents *[[read the blog here: "Skill Files, Not Code: How I Built a Human-in-the-Loop Workflow for My AI Coding Agent"](/blog/2026/discovery-on-how-i-build-ai-agent-workflow/)]*, writing technical articles, researching frameworks and programming languages, etc. Surprisingly, I found myself **genuinely enjoying software engineering again**, even though I hadn't committed to going back. 

This enjoyment happened *just because I was curious* to learn something new again. Even, around this time I built some tiny utilities that I need just to learn other ideas around it. I wanted to understand how things worked and learn more. Maybe, I found my curiosity again because I was doing things I loved (learning, solving problems, researching) without any pressure, nobody told me to do these things so here were no deadlines or any expectations to worry about. I did not have to plan my work or think about how I was doing. It was really, refreshing & fun. For the time since I started working as a software engineer **I was building things because I really wanted to not because I had to**. Although I am open to exploring roles where this engineering curiosity can be valued, my curiosity for software engineering is still there. **My curiosity, for software engineering never went away it was just hidden under all the problems that life gave me.**



## What I Actually Missed

Initially, I assumed, maybe I missed having a formal job in software engineering. But after reflecting again, I am pretty sure that wasn't entirely true at all. **I missed building things. Solving problems. And learning something new simply because I was curious.** These were the stuffs that got me into software engineering in the first place, and ironically, they were the things I didn't get to fully do when I was climbing the career ladder.

For now, *curiosity doesn't feel like pressure* at all. It feels so strangely fun. Opening your laptop just to test one small idea and accidentally spending the next four hours experimenting. Reading documentation and writing technical notes after stumbling on an interesting engineering pattern. It is refreshing to enjoy these processes naturally again, focusing on how things worked rather than just racing against the clock. At the beginning of my career break, I genuinely thought I'd lost my passion for software engineering especially when my focus shifted to my scholarship hunting process, but now I realize that **actually I just didn't have the mental space to enjoy software engineering at those moments. I didn't have the chance to enjoy it because I was trying to keep my boat afloat during an unexpected storm.**

## Closing thoughts

I still don't know exactly where this exploration will lead. Maybe I'll return to software engineering. Maybe I'll end up somewhere adjacent, where my engineering background keeps shaping how I think and solve problems. I'm keeping an open mind. At least I rediscovered about myself that **I enjoy building again. I enjoy learning again. I enjoy exploring ideas because they make me curious.** Technical skills can always be refreshed. Frameworks, tools, and programming languages will get updated as time goes. I think for *what matters for me now, is to preserve my curiosity to keep learning, experimenting, and creating.* This rediscovered curiosity will be the fuel I need to build high-impact solutions again in the future. Reconnecting with why I love engineering is exactly what sparks my drive back into the industry. And that's a good place to start.
