We love talking about linear growth in this industry. Internal promotions, steady climbs through technical roles, the neat career ladder story. I was lucky. I moved into technical leadership earlier than I'd expected myself, and for a while my career felt less like stairs and more like momentum I didn't fully choose. But **no amount of professional acceleration prepares you for the moment life hands you constraints you didn't map out**, and _forces you to slam the brakes_.

## Starting point: physical recovery and the hunt for grad school

It started with a crossroads. I was recovering from an invisible physical injury and the health complications that followed an accident in late 2024, working a high-pressure job in digital banking (serving millions of customers) and hunting for a master's scholarship at the same time. I was closing in on 30, which is where most major postgraduate scholarships cut off eligibility. So **I made the call to take a career break in mid-2025**.

The plan was simple on paper: _recover physically, and put everything into landing a Master's_ in a field I actually wanted, cybersecurity, even though my background wasn't technical enough on paper to make that an easy sell. **Life doesn't care about your plan, though.** Right as my recovery started to stabilize, a family medical emergency put me in the role of temporary core caregiver for both parents, taking turns, and at one point both of them sick at the same time, until things stabilized enough for other support to take over. My days stopped being about physiotherapy, language classes, and scholarship essays alone. _They became a mix of rehab, caregiving, and trying to keep an academic dream alive on the side._

Somehow, it worked. By the first half of 2026 I'd made it to interview stages for several competitive scholarships. On paper, the plan was working.

## The realization: shifting ground demands a shifting strategy

Standing at those interview stages in mid-2026, it hit me that **this wasn't the finish line. It was the midpoint.** And the tech world looked nothing like it did when I wrote my statement of purpose. AI, LLMs, agentic systems, the whole market moving under everyone's feet. _I stopped treating grad school as an emotional milestone and started running it through a cold ROI calculation, backed by an honest risk analysis._

The real wake-up call came from actually looking at the job market. What I found wasn't pretty. The global economy isn't in great shape right now, and it shows: **plenty of well-educated people are out of work**, and I've personally seen Master's graduates end up in jobs that have nothing to do with knowledge work or white-collar roles at all. The market's saturated, at both the Bachelor's and Master's level. _Unless you're aiming for academia, most industries don't reward advanced degrees the way they used to._ In software engineering especially, **a credential alone doesn't carry the weight it once did**. Companies want people who can _solve today's problem_, not people with the right piece of paper.

So the question became: is spending years locked into a rigid academic structure, juggling part-time work alone in a foreign country, really the best way to keep up with a field that changes weekly? The data I'd gathered was leaning no. **A traditional academic path probably wasn't going to give me the return I was looking for**, _or at least that was starting to look like the most likely outcome_.

## Overcoming the sunk cost fallacy

Walking away from something you've bled for is hard. That's the **sunk cost fallacy** doing its job: _keep going because you've already gone this far_. I'd made it to interview rounds. Quitting felt, on the surface, like tossing away a prize I'd already half-won.

But **staying the course out of stubbornness, in a field built on logic, despite data telling you otherwise, is just bad strategy**. Some of the decision-making literature I came across generally backs this up: _data beats pride_. My career wasn't going to be the exception.

Logic doesn't kill a long-held dream on its own, though. For months I sat with the numbers, second-guessing myself, wondering if I was being smart or just cynical. I needed something to turn the abstract math into something undeniable. That came recently, late at night, watching [a viral clip](https://www.tiktok.com/@republikaonline/video/7657868808115309844) from a [Constitutional Court hearing in Indonesia](https://www.tempo.co/politik/dosen-non-asn-di-mk-gaji-kecil-hingga-status-tak-jelas-2272563). A lecturer who'd earned a Master's and a PhD abroad, from well-regarded institutions, had spent over a decade teaching at a top state university. **Their monthly take-home pay: around IDR 3 million. Less than $200.**

That clip didn't make my decision for me. _It crystallized something that had already been building for months._ I still respect people who choose academia. It's a noble field, shaping the next generation. But for my specific track, **the traditional route stopped being a gamble worth taking**. It gave me the clarity to _finally cut my losses_.

So, in mid-2026, **I pivoted**. I stepped away from the scholarship track. Instead of waiting for a degree to define my next chapter, I decided to shift, gradually, putting my energy into **deliberate upskilling**, while also _experimenting with adjacent fields where my transferable skills could actually be put to work_. I kept building my portfolio in software engineering, the thing I already knew, and started another one in fields where that same skill set could carry over.

## What this taught me

1. **Five-year plans are hypotheses, not commandments.** Real career ownership means looking at _new data_ and being willing to **pivot without flinching**.

2. **Walking away isn't automatically failure.** Not if you replace the thing you dropped with _something more strategic_. **Knowing when to cut your losses** matters as much as knowing when to push through.

3. **The sunk cost isn't a total loss.** The scholarship interviews, the language classes, the discipline of writing essays around rehab and hospital visits, none of that vanished when I changed direction. It's still there, just pointed somewhere else now. _Letting go of the destination doesn't mean the road was wasted._ If anything, I've started thinking of that whole stretch as **tuition, not for a degree, but for learning how to survive and keep functioning** when life forces a version of circumstances that's far from ideal.

4. **Sometimes the most logical choice is to step back.** I was juggling recovery, a high-pressure job, caregiving, and a scholarship hunt all at once. Given how much my health mattered in all of this, **pausing in the first place was the thing that let me get through the rest of it at all**. It wasn't an easy decision, and it wasn't a relaxed one either. Once I actually sat down and weighed everything I was carrying, _stepping back was simply the most logical way to stay standing_. (I wrote more about that recovery journey itself [here](/blog/2026/what-injury-recovery-journey-taught-me/) if you want the fuller story.)

5. **Timing is out of your hands.** Plans made in calm water _rarely survive a storm_. **Growth tends to show up exactly when you're forced to adapt** to constraints you didn't choose.

6. **Good decisions can take longer to feel right than they take to calculate.** I had the numbers months before I had the conviction. _Sitting with that gap, doubting myself even after the math was already clear, wasn't wasted time._ It was just the **slower, human part of the process** catching up to the fast, logical part.

7. **There's a strange freedom in accepting that the world is chaotic and often unfair.** Some call it absurdism, others lean on nihilism or stoicism to get to a similar place. The job market doesn't owe you anything, systems don't reliably reward effort, and life doesn't check your timeline before it throws something at you. Fighting that costs energy I'd rather spend elsewhere, so **I've tried to keep my focus on what I can actually control**: _my daily effort, the skills I choose to build, the decision to pivot._

8. **None of this happens solo.** A pivot like this only works because **other people absorbed some of the weight while I figured it out**, _family, friends, whoever showed up during the parts I couldn't have managed alone._ It's easy to tell this story as a solo recalculation. It wasn't.

9. **Your worth isn't the credential.** I went into this chasing an institutional stamp of approval, something external to point to and say "see, I made it." **Letting go of the scholarship meant also letting go of needing that particular stamp to feel like the effort counted.**

10. **Credentials versus capability.** An advanced degree still matters in academia, and in a handful of niche technical fields too. Across most common, commercial white-collar industries, though, **a working portfolio and the ability to pick up new tools fast now counts for more than a diploma**. _I don't love that this is true._ But from what I've seen among people around me, it seems to be.

## Closing thoughts

I don't have every variable figured out. This wasn't the plan, it was how I found a way to keep standing through trials I never saw coming, and **turning down that personal goal to align with where the world actually stands right now was just another calculated bet** in a field where nobody has the full blueprint anyway. **Adapting on the fly is basically the whole job now**, and this is just my latest attempt at it. _If writing this down helps someone else feel a little less lost in their own version of it, that's enough._
